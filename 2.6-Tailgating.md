# Tailgating

## Introduction

Tailgating is a physical form of social engineering.

It occurs when an unauthorized person gains access to a restricted area by following an authorized person through a secured entrance.

Unlike phishing or vishing, tailgating involves physical access rather than primarily digital communication.

---

## How Does Tailgating Work?

An attacker may wait near a restricted entrance and attempt to enter when an authorized employee opens the door.

The attacker may use social interaction to avoid suspicion.

For example, they might say:

> "Could you hold the door for me? I forgot my access card."

The attacker is relying on the employee's willingness to help.

---

## Why is Tailgating Dangerous?

Physical access can provide an attacker with opportunities to:

* Access restricted areas
* Observe sensitive information
* Steal equipment
* Connect unauthorized devices
* Access computers or network equipment
* Obtain confidential documents

---

## Tailgating vs. Piggybacking

The terms **tailgating** and **piggybacking** are sometimes used interchangeably, but there can be a distinction.

**Tailgating** generally describes unauthorized access obtained by following someone without their permission.

**Piggybacking** can describe a situation where the authorized person knowingly allows the other person to enter.

Both situations can create physical security risks.

---

## How to Prevent Tailgating

Organizations can reduce the risk by:

* Using access cards or badges.
* Installing security doors.
* Educating employees.
* Using security guards where appropriate.
* Monitoring entrances.
* Using visitor identification procedures.
* Avoiding unauthorized entry.
* Challenging unfamiliar people in restricted areas according to organizational procedures.

---

## Conclusion

Tailgating demonstrates that cybersecurity also includes physical security.

Protecting computers and networks is not enough if unauthorized people can physically access sensitive areas or equipment.
